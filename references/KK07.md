# Noisy binary search and its applications

*Summary [KK07] · R. M. Karp and R. Kleinberg, SODA 2007, 881–890.*

*Tags: black-box evaluation · statistics · probability · complexity theory.*

*Summarized by: Claude 5 Fable directed by Lionel Levine.*

**TL;DR.** Binary search when every comparison is a coin toss: given $n$ coins whose unknown heads probabilities increase along the sequence, locate where they cross a target value $\tau$, observing one toss per query. Karp and Kleinberg introduced this monotone-probabilities model and determined its cost: $\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)$ tosses are necessary and sufficient to locate the crossing up to a tolerance of $\varepsilon$ in probability. The bound is the information-theoretic one — a toss of a coin whose bias sits within $\varepsilon$ of $\tau$ reveals $O(\varepsilon^2)$ bits, and the answer takes $\log n$ bits to name.

## Setting

Classical binary search inserts an element into an ordered list with $\log_2 n$ comparisons. Suppose the comparisons cannot be made directly: each position $i$ carries a coin of unknown heads probability $p_i$, the only structure is monotonicity $p_1\le\dots\le p_n$, and a query tosses one chosen coin once. Given a target $\tau$, the task is to find the position where the sequence crosses $\tau$ — the highest-numbered coin with heads probability below the target. Since coins with $p_i$ arbitrarily close to $\tau$ are statistically indistinguishable from it, the answer is only demanded up to a tolerance: a position where the sequence's crossing of $\tau$ is resolved to within $\varepsilon$ in probability. The model generalizes noisy binary search with a fixed error rate (each comparison's answer flipped independently with probability $\zeta$), which is the special case where every $p_i$ is either $\zeta$ or $1-\zeta$, with tolerance $\varepsilon=\tfrac12-\zeta$.

## Main results

1. **Query complexity.** $\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)$ tosses are necessary and sufficient to locate the crossing to tolerance $\varepsilon$: an adaptive algorithm achieving the upper bound, and a matching information-theoretic lower bound.
2. **Fixed-noise scaling.** In the flipped-answer special case the bound reads $O(\log n/(\tfrac12-\zeta)^2)$, matching the Shannon capacity scaling of the binary symmetric channel, $1-H(\zeta)\asymp(\tfrac12-\zeta)^2$ for $\zeta$ near $\tfrac12$. (The sharp leading constant $\log_2 n/(1-H(\zeta))$ is later work, Ben-Or–Hassidim 2008.)
3. **Applications.** The paper applies the search primitive to sequential decision problems where a threshold in an ordered family must be found from Bernoulli feedback, among them investment planning, sponsored-search advertising, and admission control in queueing networks.

## Method

The algorithm is Bayesian in spirit: maintain weights over the candidate crossing positions, toss a coin chosen at the weighted middle, and reweight multiplicatively according to the outcome; tosses far from the crossing are decisively biased and eliminate half the weight quickly, while tosses near it are nearly fair and contribute the $\varepsilon^{-2}$ factor. The lower bound is a channel-capacity count: identifying one of $n$ positions requires $\log n$ bits, and each toss near the target yields $O(\varepsilon^2)$.

## Why it matters for AI safety

Behavioral tomography reconstructs an agent's causal world model from sampled actions alone. Its one-dimensional core is exactly a noisy search problem: at zero regret an optimal policy is deterministic away from a switching surface, so locating the surface is bisection on noiseless bits; positive regret and response corruption smear the answers into Bernoulli observations that are monotone across the surface — Karp–Kleinberg's setting. Their bound supplies one endpoint of the interpolation between bisection and Bernoulli regression, and the channel-capacity cost $1-H(\zeta)$ per corrupted action enters the conjectured budgets directly. The coupled, many-dimensional causal reconstruction built on this primitive is the subject of [MAIS-A2](../agendas/A2/).
