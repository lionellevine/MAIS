# Incompleteness in the finite domain

*Summary [P17] · P. Pudlák, Bulletin of Symbolic Logic 23 (2017), no. 4, 405–441 · [arXiv:1601.01487](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.01487).*

*Tags: logic · complexity theory · proof-based agents · bounded rationality.*

*Summarized by: Claude Fable 5 directed by Lionel Levine.*

**TL;DR.** Gödel's theorems say certain sentences have no proofs; their finite-domain shadows ask how *long* the proofs of their restricted instances must be. The anchor result, due to Friedman and Pudlák, concerns the finite consistency statement $\mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ ("no contradiction in $S$ has a proof of at most $n$ symbols"): for a large class of theories, every $S$-proof of $\mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ has more than $n^{\varepsilon}$ symbols, while $n^{c}$ symbols suffice. The survey builds a program around such statements — provable instances, unprovable uniformizations, proof-length bounds attached — and puts forward conjectures going beyond $\mathsf{NP}\neq\mathsf{coNP}$ that tie the computational complexity of a problem associated with a sentence to the sentence's unprovability in weak theories, or to the length of its proofs.

## Setting

Fix a recursively axiomatized theory $S$ from a large class that includes $\mathsf{PA}$. Gödel's second theorem makes $\mathrm{Con}(S)$ unprovable in $S$, but each finite instance $\mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ is a true, decidable statement, and $S$ proves it. The quantitative question is the least number of symbols in an $S$-proof of $\mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ as a function of $n$. More broadly, the finite-domain program takes a classical incompleteness phenomenon (an unprovable universal sentence whose instances are provable) and asks for the growth rate of the instance proofs — the shape of statement that carries all the information the unbounded theorem discards.

## Main results

1. **The Gödel corner lies between two polynomials (Friedman, Pudlák; surveyed here).** There is $\varepsilon>0$ such that every $S$-proof of $\mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ has more than $n^{\varepsilon}$ symbols, and there is $c$ such that $S \vdash \mathrm{Con}_S(n)$ in at most $n^{c}$ symbols. The exponents do not match, and closing that gap is open. For calculi with Rosser's C-rule the lower bound improves to $\Omega(n/\log^2 n)$, a 1987 result of Pudlák reported in this survey.
2. **The conjectures.** Conjecture $\mathrm{CON}^{N+}$ asserts that for theories $S,T$ in the class, if $T$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(S)$ then the $S$-proofs of $\mathrm{Con}_T(n)$ are not polynomially bounded; the paper's Proposition 3.2 makes the instance $T=S+\mathrm{Con}(S)$ pivotal. This is one member of a family formalizing a general thesis: high computational complexity of the problem attached to a sentence should force the sentence to be unprovable in a weak theory, or to require long proofs.
3. **Systematization.** The paper reviews earlier conjectures, introduces new ones, organizes them, and proves new implications between them and previously studied statements in proof complexity.

## Method

The engine of the Friedman–Pudlák lower bounds is Parikh's self-referential sentence "I have no proof of at most $n$ symbols": a short sentence whose shortest proof is provably long. The upper bounds come from efficient arithmetization — carrying out the soundness argument for length-$n$ proofs inside $S$ with only polynomial overhead. The bridge to computational complexity runs through propositional translations, which convert proof-length questions for first-order theories into lower-bound questions for propositional proof systems, where they sit just beyond $\mathsf{NP}\neq\mathsf{coNP}$.

## Why it matters for AI safety

Incompleteness is usually an all-or-nothing statement; this survey develops its finite-scale refinement, where the question is not whether a system can prove its own consistency but how long a proof of "no contradiction of length $n$" must be. That finite-scale question is the proved corner of the problem that prices cooperation between transparent agents: the Löbian overhead $F_S(k,n)$ of [MAIS-O1](../open-problems/MAIS-O1.md) — the cost of converting a proof of the reflection instance $\Box P \to P$ into a proof of $P$ — specializes at $P=\bot$ to the length of proofs of finite consistency, exactly the quantity the Friedman–Pudlák bounds settle up to the exponent. Pudlák's survey also supplies the frame: statements with provable instances, unprovable uniformizations, and proof-length bounds attached are exactly the shape of the agenda's bounded reflection principles, and its length-tracked lower-bound techniques are the ones a lower bound on $F$ would have to extend. For the audit that places these bounds inside the overhead problem, see [MAIS-A1](../agendas/A1/).
