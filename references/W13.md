# A widely applicable Bayesian information criterion

*Summary [W13] · S. Watanabe, Journal of Machine Learning Research 14 (2013), 867–897 · [arXiv:1208.6338](https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.6338).*

*Tags: singular learning theory · developmental interpretability · interpretability · statistics · probability.*

*Summarized by: Claude Fable 5 directed by Lionel Levine.*

**TL;DR.** Schwarz's BIC fails for singular models. Watanabe's replacement, WBIC, averages empirical loss under a posterior tempered to inverse temperature $1/\log n$. For singular and unrealizable models it recovers the free energy's $nL_n(w_0)$ and $\lambda\log n$ terms, with discrepancy of order $\sqrt{\log n}$ in probability, and can be estimated from one Monte Carlo temperature without knowing the true distribution.

## Setting

Given $n$ i.i.d. samples, a model $p(y \mid x, w)$ on a compact parameter set $W$, and a smooth positive prior $\varphi$, write $L_n(w) = -\frac1n \sum_i \log p(Y_i \mid X_i, w)$ for the empirical loss. The **Bayes free energy** is $F_n = -\log \int_W \exp(-n L_n(w))\, \varphi(w)\, dw$, the fundamental quantity of Bayesian model selection. In singular models — where the optimal parameters form a set with singularities rather than a point with invertible Fisher information — Watanabe's earlier theory [[W09]](W09.md) gives $F_n = n L_n(w_0) + \lambda \log n - (m-1) \log\log n + O_p(1)$, with $\lambda$ the (global) **learning coefficient** and $m$ its multiplicity. The catch: $F_n$ itself is expensive to compute, and $\lambda$ requires resolving the singularities of the model. The **tempered posterior** at inverse temperature $\beta > 0$ is proportional to $\exp(-n\beta L_n(w))\, \varphi(w)$, and
$$\mathrm{WBIC} = \mathbb{E}^{\beta^\ast}[\, n L_n(w) \,], \qquad \beta^\ast = \frac{1}{\log n}.$$

## Main results

1. **Main theorem.** Under the hypotheses of singular learning theory, $\mathrm{WBIC} = n L_n(w_0) + \lambda \log n + U_n \sqrt{\lambda \log n / 2} + O_p(1)$, where $U_n$ is a random sequence converging in law to a Gaussian. So WBIC reproduces the free energy up to $O_p(\sqrt{\log n})$: same leading term, same coefficient $\lambda$.
2. **The temperature is canonical.** There is a unique inverse temperature $\beta_n$ at which the tempered expectation of $n L_n$ equals $F_n$ exactly, and $\beta_n \log n \to 1$ in probability. The choice $\beta^\ast = 1/\log n$ is forced, not a convenience.
3. **Regular case.** For regular models $\lambda = d/2$, and WBIC recovers Schwarz's BIC.

The practical import: earlier routes to $F_n$ (thermodynamic integration) need Monte Carlo runs at many temperatures; WBIC needs one run at one temperature, and no access to the truth.

## Proof method

Resolution of singularities puts the population loss in normal crossing form in local charts, so the tempered-posterior integral becomes a Laplace-type integral whose scale is read off from the poles of a zeta function: the largest pole $-\lambda$, of order $m$, dictates the $\lambda \log n$ term. An empirical-process argument on the renormalized likelihood ratio controls the fluctuations and yields the Gaussian $\sqrt{\lambda \log n / 2}$ correction. This is the machinery of [[W09]](W09.md), run at inverse temperature $1/\log n$ instead of $1$.

## Why it matters for AI safety

Developmental interpretability estimates local learning coefficients inside trained networks as a handle on which internal mechanism a network has learned; the estimator of Lau, Furman, Wang, Murfet, and Wei [[LFWMW23]](LFWMW23.md) is WBIC's numerator with a Gaussian localization term added, and this theorem is what proves anything about where that estimator converges. In [MAIS-A6](../agendas/A6/) the theorem cuts both ways. With fixed localization strength the Gaussian pin is just another smooth positive prior, so the theorem forces convergence to the *global* coefficient — the estimator leaks to the most singular point ([MAIS-O69](../open-problems/MAIS-O69.md)). Restricted instead to a small ball around the point of interest, the same theorem gives convergence to the *local* coefficient, which is what makes ground-truth calibration tables meaningful ([MAIS-O70](../open-problems/MAIS-O70.md)). The open question — how fast must the pin tighten with $n$ to cross from one regime to the other — is Problem 6.1 of [MAIS-A6](../agendas/A6/).
