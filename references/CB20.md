# Implicit bias of gradient descent for wide two-layer neural networks trained with the logistic loss

*Summary [CB20] · L. Chizat and F. Bach, COLT 2020 · [arXiv:2002.04486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04486).*

*Tags: simplicity bias · training dynamics · optimization · generalization.*

*Summarized by: Claude 5 Fable directed by Lionel Levine.*

**TL;DR.** Gradient descent on an overparameterized network does not just fit the data — it selects one particular interpolant, and this paper says which. For infinitely wide two-layer networks with homogeneous activations trained by gradient flow on the logistic loss, the limiting classifier (when the flow converges in direction) is the maximum-margin classifier of a *non-Hilbertian* function space: margin is measured per unit variation norm, not per unit kernel norm. That norm adapts to hidden low-dimensional structure in the data, so the resulting margin — and the generalization bound it yields — is independent of the ambient dimension, in contrast to the kernel support vector machine implicitly solved when only the output layer is trained.

## Setting

The network is a two-layer net with positively homogeneous activation (ReLU is the motivating case), taken in the mean-field normalization: as the width goes to infinity the network becomes an integral over a probability measure on neurons, and training becomes a Wasserstein gradient flow on that measure — the infinite-width dynamics established in the authors' earlier global-convergence work. The loss is the logistic loss, and more generally any loss with exponential tails; because such losses have no minimizer at finite norm, the parameters diverge and the meaningful object is the *direction* of the predictor. The function-space yardstick is the **variation norm**: the least value of $\int|a|\,\|u\|$ over all measures representing the function — the unit ball of the space usually called $\mathcal F_1$, strictly larger than any single reproducing-kernel ball.

## Main results

1. **Max-margin characterization.** Under a convergence assumption on the flow (weak convergence of the normalized representing measures), the limiting predictor maximizes the training margin over the unit ball of the variation norm. The implicit bias of rich-regime training is thereby identified with a concrete convex optimization problem in function space.
2. **Adaptivity and generalization.** When the data have hidden low-dimensional structure, the variation-norm margin is independent of the ambient dimension, which converts into dimension-free generalization bounds for the trained network.
3. **Kernel contrast.** Training only the output layer implicitly solves a kernel support vector machine — max margin in a Hilbert ball — which enjoys no such adaptivity. The gap between the two margins is the paper's measure of what training the hidden layer buys.
4. **Computation.** The analysis of training time is non-quantitative in general; in simplified settings the authors prove computational guarantees by exhibiting an equivalence with online mirror descent. Numerical experiments with ReLU networks match the theory.

## Method

The engine is the mean-field description of two-layer training as a Wasserstein gradient flow over neuron space. Homogeneity makes the predictor's norm grow without bound under an exponentially tailed loss, so the authors pass to normalized dynamics and characterize the limit points: any directional limit must satisfy the optimality conditions of the variation-norm margin program, and the smoothness of the logistic loss lets first-order stationarity be upgraded to global max-margin optimality. The convergence of the normalized flow itself is a hypothesis, not a theorem — the result is a characterization of the limit granted that it exists.

## Why it matters for AI safety

In the coin-line environment of [MAIS-A8](../agendas/A8/), two training-indistinguishable policies — the proxy "move right" and the intended "go to the coin" — compete for selection by gradient descent, and this theorem decides the contest in the rich regime: the agenda's mean-field proposition computes the variation-norm margin maximizer for the coin line, finds it is unique, and finds it takes the proxy action at the probe state. The theorem is what turns "what does an infinitely wide network learn here?" into a finite convex problem one can solve by hand. Its convergence hypothesis is also exactly the unproven premise that separates the agenda's infinite-width propositions from its finite-width conjectures: whether standard-parameterization training at large finite width obeys the same verdict is [MAIS-O82](../open-problems/MAIS-O82.md), and whether anything in the standard family escapes it is [MAIS-O83](../open-problems/MAIS-O83.md).
