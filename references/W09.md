# Algebraic Geometry and Statistical Learning Theory

*Summary [W09] · S. Watanabe, Cambridge Monographs on Applied and Computational Mathematics 25, Cambridge University Press, 2009.*

*Tags: singular learning theory · algebraic geometry · generalization · statistics · developmental interpretability.*

*Summarized by: Claude Fable 5 directed by Lionel Levine.*

**TL;DR.** When a statistical model's set of true parameters is a singular variety — the generic situation for neural networks, mixtures, and other nonidentifiable models — the classical BIC asymptotics fail, and this book supplies their replacement: the Bayes free energy expands as $nS_n + \lambda \log n - (m-1)\log\log n$, and the expected generalization error as $\lambda/n$, where $\lambda$ is the real log canonical threshold of the population loss — a rational number computed by resolving the singularities of the loss, in the sense of algebraic geometry — and $m$ is its multiplicity. Half the parameter count, the regular-model penalty, is demoted to an upper bound; the complexity measure that learning theory actually charges is a birational invariant of the loss landscape.

## Setting

A statistical model is a family of densities $p(x \mid w)$ (or conditional densities $p(y \mid x, w)$) with parameter $w$ in a compact set $W \subset \mathbb R^d$ defined by finitely many analytic inequalities; data are drawn i.i.d.\ from a fixed truth $q$. The central object is the population loss $K(w)$, the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from the truth to the model at $w$, which vanishes precisely on the set $W_0$ of parameters reproducing the truth. Given $n$ samples and a smooth positive prior, the free energy $F_n$ is minus the log of the integrated likelihood, and the generalization error $G_n$ is the divergence from the truth to the posterior predictive density. The hypotheses of the main theorems: $K$ extends to a nonzero real-analytic function near $W$, the truth is realizable ($W_0 \neq \emptyset$), and the log-likelihood ratio has relatively finite variance. A model is *regular* when the optimum is unique with positive-definite Hessian; then Laplace approximation gives the textbook penalty $\frac d2 \log n$. The book's subject is every other case: when $W_0$ is a positive-dimensional, typically singular analytic set, the Fisher information degenerates and the Laplace argument has nothing to expand around.

## Main results

1. **Free energy.** There exist a rational $\lambda \in (0, d/2]$ and an integer $m \in \{1,\dots,d\}$, depending only on the loss, domain, and prior, with $F_n = nS_n + \lambda \log n - (m-1)\log\log n + O_p(1)$, where $S_n$ is the empirical entropy of the sample. Regular models recover $\lambda = d/2$, $m = 1$; smaller $\lambda$ means a more degenerate optimal set and a smaller effective complexity, so the expansion refines BIC.
2. **Generalization.** The expected generalization error obeys $\mathbb E[G_n] = \lambda/n + o(1/n)$: the same invariant is both the complexity penalty in the free energy and the coefficient of $1/n$ in the generalization error, usurping both roles the parameter count plays for regular models.
3. **Identification of $(\lambda, m)$.** The zeta function $\zeta(z) = \int_W K(w)^z \varphi(w)\,dw$ continues meromorphically to $\mathbb C$ with poles at negative rational numbers (by Atiyah's argument via resolution of singularities); $-\lambda$ is the largest pole and $m$ its order. This $\lambda$ is the **real log canonical threshold** of $K$. Equivalently, the prior volume of the almost-true parameters scales as $\operatorname{vol}\{K \le \varepsilon\} \asymp \varepsilon^{\lambda}(\log \frac1\varepsilon)^{m-1}$: the learning coefficient is a volume-growth exponent, answering "how plentiful are the parameters that fit the truth to within $\varepsilon$?"

## Method

Everything rests on Hironaka's resolution of singularities, in the real-analytic form of Chapter 3: near each point of $W_0$ there is a proper map $g$ from a manifold, an isomorphism away from the zero set, in whose local coordinates $K(g(u))$ becomes a monomial $u_1^{2k_1}\cdots u_d^{2k_d}$ and the Jacobian a monomial times a positive factor. In such coordinates the zeta integral is elementary — each chart contributes candidate poles $-(h_j+1)/(2k_j)$, and $\lambda$ is the minimum over charts (Chapter 4). On the resolved manifold the log-likelihood ratio admits a standard form, its fluctuations controlled by an empirical process, and the free-energy integral becomes a Laplace-type integral whose $n$-asymptotics are read off the pole structure of $\zeta$. Computing $\lambda$ for a concrete model is exactly as hard as finding, or circumventing, the resolution.

## Why it matters for AI safety

Two networks can fit the same data by different internal mechanisms and then behave differently on inputs no test anticipated. Watanabe's theorem makes "which mechanism does learning select" a mathematical question: the posterior concentrates on the parts of the optimal set with the smallest learning coefficient, so $\lambda$ is a quantitative handle on the representations Bayesian learning prefers, and an active experimental program now estimates local learning coefficients inside networks with billions of parameters. What is missing is ground truth and a bridge to gradient descent: exact values of $\lambda$ are known only for a short list of classical models, and the selection principle is a theorem only for the Bayesian posterior, not for training dynamics. Those are the subjects of [MAIS-A6](../agendas/A6/) and [MAIS-A7](../agendas/A7/).
